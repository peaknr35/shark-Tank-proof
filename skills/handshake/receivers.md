# What each receiver needs to see

Every pitch starts with the same three sizes, built only from claims that pass
the gate for this audience:

- **One sentence:** "For [who], [business] [does what] so they can [result]."
- **Three sentences:** the problem in their terms, what exists today, the proof
  and the ask. Planned results are said as goals ("we are running pilots to
  test a 50% drop"), never as results.
- **Three minutes:** the problem, who has it, what you built (shown running if
  possible), your proof, what you want next.

Then the format for the receiver.

## A customer (a pilot, an audit, a first order)

They say yes to their own before and after, not to a finished product. The
first yes is small and time-boxed. A big upfront fee from a stranger with no
references is the most common reason a good B2B idea hears no.

**One-page pilot offer:**
1. Their problem, in their words (from a real call if you have one).
2. Their number today, in their units. If you only have an estimate, say so
   and make measuring it step one of the pilot.
3. What you will run, on what, for how long (two to eight weeks).
4. What done looks like to them, and how you will both measure it. Write the
   success line before the pilot starts.
5. What could go wrong, said plainly, and what happens then.
6. The price: paid, a deposit credited to the first month, or free in exchange
   for named things (staff time for check-ins, their before and after numbers,
   a quote and a case study if it works). Say what happens at the end: roll
   into the standard price, or remove it and owe nothing.

**Good proof:** a similar case, a measured result, a quote, a short demo on
their kind of data.

## A funder (a grant, a seed fund)

They fund a question, a method and a number. Nobody expects a finished product.

**NSF Project Pitch, four sections** (check the current limits on
seedfund.nsf.gov before you write; these were the limits last checked):
1. The Technology Innovation: what is new about the approach. (3,500 characters)
2. The Technical Objectives and Challenges: what you will test, how, and what
   could go wrong. Your tests and failures belong here. (3,500 characters)
3. The Market Opportunity: who has the pain, and what it costs them. Every
   market number needs a source or the word "estimate". (1,750 characters)
4. The Company and Team: who you are, what you have done, and your partners.
   Only real advisors and partners, named only with their yes. (1,750 characters)

Most grant calls and RFPs ask for these same four things in their own words.
Read the call and answer in its headings.

**Good proof:** a method someone else could follow, early results, partners (a
university or institution helps), the people who would use it.

## An investor

They buy a believable path to a large outcome. They have seen every simulated
projection before; real pull is what they look for.

**One-pager:** the problem and who has it, what exists today, the real evidence
(paid pilots, retention, revenue, with dates), the market with sources, the
model (`PLANNED`, labelled), the team, the ask and what it buys in months.

**Good proof:** revenue or signed pilots, growth week on week, a named customer
who will take a reference call.

## A lender or landlord

They want to be paid back. They read the cash, not the story.

**One page:** what the business is, the startup spend and where it goes, the
cash needed and from whom, the break-even and the ramp (`PLANNED`, with the
what-ifs), what is already real (quotes, a lease offer, pre-orders), and the
founder's own money in.

## Someone inside your business (a boss, leadership, another department)

They approve when they can see time or money back for the team, and low risk.

**One-page business case:**
1. The job today: who does it, how often, how long it takes.
2. What you built and tried, and on what.
3. The before and after, in hours or money.
4. Risks (data, errors, security) and how you handle them.
5. The ask: time, budget, access, or one team to try it.

**Good proof:** a run on real (or realistic) work, a colleague who used it, a
number.

## The people who will use it

They say yes when it runs for them without you there.

**Invite message plus one-page guide:**
1. What it does for them, in one sentence.
2. How to start: the link, and the first thing to type or upload.
3. What good output looks like.
4. Who to tell when it breaks.

**Good proof:** their own runs, their feedback, how often they come back.
